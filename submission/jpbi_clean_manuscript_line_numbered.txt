0001	# Multimodal Cancer Drug-Response Prediction Shows Small, Validation-Dependent Gains in a 15-Compound DepMap Benchmark
0002	
0003	# Abstract
0004	
0005	Background: Adding molecular assays may improve drug-response prediction, but the benefit may depend on the response resource, validation target, and data completeness. Methods: I compared expression-only and early-fusion Ridge models using expression, copy-number, and damaging-mutation features from GDSC1, GDSC2, and CTD². The declared benchmark contained 15 compounds. Feature selection, imputation, and scaling were performed within each training fold. I evaluated five grouped drug-held-out splits and leave-one-dataset-out transfer. Results: Across the five drug-held-out splits, fusion had mean RMSE 0.2322 versus 0.2328 for expression alone, a mean difference of -0.0006. Fusion favored three splits and expression favored two. In the paired drug-group audit, the mean fusion-minus-expression difference was -0.0009 with a sample SD of 0.0019 across 15 drug-seed cells. Fusion was less favorable within each of the three source-held-out folds, although these folds change source, compound composition, cell lines, assay context, and response construction together. Conclusions: The small within-universe difference is conditional and variable, and the current design does not establish a general advantage for multimodal fusion. The results support using source-aware validation and data-availability audits before adopting additional assays in early drug-discovery modeling.
0006	
0007	**Keywords:** information fusion; drug response; multi-omics; cancer; missing data; cross-study validation
0008	
0009	# 1. Introduction
0010	
0011	Cancer pharmacogenomics increasingly combines multiple molecular assays to predict drug response. More measurements can capture complementary biology, but they also increase dimensionality, missingness, and sensitivity to study-specific measurement processes. I therefore specified a workflow that treats incremental predictive value, missingness, and modality conflict as joint evaluation targets.
0012	
0013	## 1.1. Brief literature context
0014	
0015	Drug response is a heterogeneous phenotype shaped by lineage, genomic alterations, transcriptional state, assay conditions, and drug-specific mechanisms. The Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia established a large-scale link between cancer cell-line molecular profiles and pharmacologic sensitivity [1]. The Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer subsequently provided a broad pharmacogenomic map across drugs and cell lines, showing that response associations are distributed across tissue context and molecular features rather than being reducible to a single biomarker class [2]. DepMap and related resources further consolidated molecular and functional measurements for systematic cancer-model analysis [3]. These resources made computational response prediction possible, but they also made the data-integration problem more visible: assays are not uniformly measured across all models, and response data are generated under source-specific experimental protocols.
0016	
0017	The literature contains several complementary modeling strategies. Expression-only models have often been competitive because transcriptomic state captures pathway activity and lineage context, whereas mutation and copy-number features can provide sparse but mechanistically informative signals. Multimodal models combine these signals through early concatenation, late integration, multitask learning, or biologically structured architectures. MOLI, for example, integrates expression, copy-number, and mutation information through modality-specific encoders and reported gains in external validation [5]. DrugCell uses a biologically structured neural network and drug representations to model response and synergy [8], while other deep-learning frameworks have reported strong performance in cell-line settings and more variable transfer to clinical or external cohorts [9,10]. Recent reviews and benchmarking frameworks additionally emphasize drug representations, missing-modality handling, broader metrics, and clinically relevant validation designs [11-13]. These results motivate multimodal integration, but they do not imply that adding an assay is beneficial under every population, response definition, or validation design.
0018	
0019	There are three reasons to be cautious when comparing such results. First, the prediction target can change from continuous viability or IC50 to a binary responder label, making error metrics and biological interpretations non-equivalent [4,5]. Second, random splitting of cell-line-drug pairs can place related observations, or the same cell-line molecular profile, on both sides of a split; this answers a different question from transfer to a new drug or a new study. Recent work has explicitly identified leakage risks from random tuple splits in drug-response modeling [9]. Third, feature selection and preprocessing performed before validation can leak information from held-out observations even when model fitting itself is separated. These concerns are particularly important in high-dimensional multi-omics data, where dimensionality and preprocessing choices materially affect model behavior [6,7].
0020	
0021	Published comparisons therefore leave a practical gap: it remains difficult to tell whether a reported multimodal gain reflects complementary biology, a favorable split, source-specific assay coverage, or preprocessing choices. Cross-study transfer is also less frequently treated as a primary test than within-dataset cross-validation, despite differences in response scales, cell-line coverage, and molecular measurement between screening resources. I address this gap with a deliberately modest benchmark: the same Ridge learner, the same feature budget, fold-local selection from the full raw modality universe, explicit single-modality baselines, drug-held-out evaluation, and leave-one-dataset-out transfer. The aim is not to outperform deep-learning systems, but to estimate the incremental value of adding modalities under a transparent and leakage-controlled comparison.
0022	
0023	The practical question is therefore not whether a modality is biologically informative in isolation, but whether it adds reliable information after the strongest available baseline and the data-construction rules have been fixed. An additional block may improve predictions because it captures complementary biology, because it changes the effective regularization of the learner, or because it changes which rows and features are usable. These explanations have different implications for deployment. A gain that depends on complete-case filtering, a particular response scale, or a shared source protocol may not survive when the model is moved to another screening resource. Conversely, a small gain that persists under entity-level holdout could be useful even if it does not imply a mechanistic contribution from every included feature.
0024	
0025	This distinction also matters for interpreting negative results. A fusion model that fails to improve a compact expression baseline does not show that copy-number or mutation data are irrelevant. It may indicate that the chosen learner, feature budget, imputation rule, or response universe did not convert those measurements into additional predictive accuracy. The appropriate conclusion is consequently conditional: the benchmark estimates incremental value under a declared construction, rather than ranking the biological importance of the assays themselves. I designed the analysis around that narrower estimand.
0026	
0027	## The workflow is intended as a decision-support and benchmarking tool for early drug-discovery data science. In an assay-prioritization setting, a team with broad expression coverage but incomplete copy-number or mutation data can use the comparison to ask whether harmonizing or acquiring additional assays is likely to provide a gain large enough to justify its cost and maintenance burden. In biomarker-model development, the same procedure provides a check against adopting a complex multimodal model when a strong expression-only baseline performs similarly. In external-validation planning, the contrast between within-universe and source-held-out results illustrates why a model should be tested across response resources before being described as portable. The availability audit can also serve as data-integration quality control by showing whether adding a modality changes the population of usable cell lines. Finally, persisted split manifests, fold-local preprocessing, and per-compound reporting provide a reproducible benchmarking pattern that can be reused for future model comparisons.
0028	
0029	These uses are deliberately operational rather than clinical. The study does not support prediction for an arbitrary new drug, patient-treatment selection, causal claims about mutation or copy-number biology, a universal ranking of fusion versus expression, replacement of experimental validation, or clinical, manufacturing, or regulatory decisions. Those applications would require drug-aware inputs, stronger external validation, prospective experiments, and task-specific evidence.
0030	
0031	1.2. Research questions and hypotheses
0032	
0033	I asked three questions: (Q1) Does feature-level fusion improve prediction when drugs, rather than cell lines alone, are held out? (Q2) Does any improvement transfer when an entire response dataset is held out? (Q3) Are fusion effects accompanied by structured missingness or modality redundancy? I specified the following directional expectations before interpreting the results: H1, fusion may improve drug-heldout prediction relative to expression alone; H2, fusion gains may not transfer across datasets; and H3, missingness and redundancy may vary across source datasets and coincide with unstable fusion effects. These are benchmark hypotheses, not claims of biological causality.
0034	
0035	# 2. Methods
0036	
0037	I harmonized DepMap response and molecular data from GDSC1, GDSC2, and CTD² using release identifiers recorded in the repository manifest. The response variable was the source-provided AUC value; response scales were retained by source and were not interpreted as identical assay measurements. Expression, copy-number, and damaging-mutation features were evaluated separately and in concatenated early-fusion models. Within each held-out fold, the top 2,000 features per modality were selected from the full raw modality universe using training rows only. Models used training-fold median imputation, training-fold standardization, and Ridge regression with alpha = 1.0. Drug-held-out splits grouped rows by drug; leave-one-dataset-out folds held out each source dataset in turn. Outer joins were retained for structured missingness analyses.
0038	
0039	## 2.1. Evaluation scope
0040	
0041	The declared evaluation universe contains 15 compounds: five from GDSC1, five from GDSC2, and five from CTD². This is a declared cross-dataset subset, not the complete drug universe available in any one portal release. The exact compound identifiers, source-specific row counts, and cell-line counts are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Because the datasets do not contain a broad common set of identical compounds, leave-one-dataset-out results measure cross-study transfer rather than same-drug replication.
0042	
0043	## 2.2. Metrics and interpretation
0044	
0045	I report mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE). Fusion deltas are defined as fusion RMSE minus expression RMSE; negative values favor fusion and positive values favor expression. Because response distributions and assay scales differ among GDSC1, GDSC2, and CTD², absolute RMSE and MAE are interpreted only within the corresponding dataset and split. I do not use the LOOD RMSE values to rank intrinsic dataset difficulty or to claim that one source is easier or harder than another. Cross-dataset comparisons are limited to the direction and within-fold change from expression to fusion. Missingness strata are descriptive availability strata, not a replacement for imputation-aware model evaluation. All formal result tables and split manifests are versioned with the workflow.
0046	
0047	## 2.3. Dataset harmonization and response definition
0048	
0049	The workflow treats the response table as the analysis backbone and joins molecular features through stable cell-line identifiers. Drug identifiers were normalized within source datasets before defining the declared compound universe. Nominally similar compounds across sources were not assumed to be interchangeable; source-specific response measurements remained associated with their originating dataset.
0050	
0051	## 2.4. Feature construction and leakage control
0052	
0053	The 15 compounds were defined as a declared analysis subset of five compounds from each source-specific response table. The subset was inherited from the prepared benchmark tables and was not selected after inspecting held-out performance. Five compounds were retained from each dataset to provide a balanced source-level scope; this is not intended to represent the complete drug universe or a random sample of all available compounds. Exact source identifiers and response-row counts are provided in Supplementary Table S1.
0054	
0055	Cell lines were matched through the stable DepMap identifier (`depmap_id`) present in the response and molecular tables. Display names were retained for auditability but were not used as the primary join key. For each source-specific response record, molecular blocks were joined by this identifier. Missing modality rows were retained in outer-join availability audits and were handled by model preprocessing rather than silently discarded from the structural missingness analysis.
0056	
0057	Expression and copy-number blocks were represented by their numeric feature columns. The damaging-mutation matrix was used as supplied in the public DepMap release: each feature column represents a gene-level damaging-mutation indicator in the prepared matrix, with absent or non-damaging status represented as zero where encoded by the source table and unavailable values retained as missing until training-fold imputation. No additional variant pathogenicity model was fitted in this study; the analysis therefore evaluates the supplied damaging-mutation encoding rather than a new variant-classification procedure.
0058	
0059	Feature selection was performed separately for each modality inside every training fold. Candidate columns were restricted to the numeric feature universe after excluding the seven metadata columns. Features were ranked using training-fold variance, and the top 2,000 features per modality were retained. The test fold was never used for ranking. Fusion concatenated the three independently selected 2,000-feature blocks, giving a nominal 6,000-feature input before model fitting.
0060	
0061	For each selected feature, missing values were imputed using the training-fold median. The same fitted medians were applied to the corresponding test rows. Features were then standardized using training-fold means and standard deviations, with the fitted transformation applied unchanged to the test fold. Rows were not imputed using quantities calculated from held-out observations.
0062	
0063	The estimator was ordinary Ridge regression with an L2 penalty and alpha = 1.0, fit separately for each modality and for the concatenated fusion block. This alpha was fixed for all comparisons and was not selected using held-out responses. The fixed learner provides a transparent ablation of modality addition; it is not presented as an optimized or universally superior model specification.
0064	
0065	The model contains no drug identity, chemical structure, target, dose, or other drug-related feature. It therefore cannot learn a drug-specific response function in the usual sense and should not be described as a conventional predictor for an arbitrary unseen compound. In the grouped drug-held-out analysis, the model applies a cell-line molecular response relationship learned from the remaining declared compounds to held-out compound records. This is a limited stress test of transfer within the selected response universe, not evidence that molecular features alone can predict any new drug without drug-related information. A drug-aware model with explicit compound descriptors is a future extension.
0066	
0067	Expression, copy-number, and damaging-mutation matrices were processed as separate modality blocks. Feature selection was repeated inside every training fold using only training-row variance and the declared feature budget. Selected blocks were imputed and standardized using training-derived quantities before model fitting; test rows were transformed with those fitted quantities. This ordering prevents held-out response information from influencing feature selection or preprocessing.
0068	
0069	## 2.5. Model specification and baselines
0070	
0071	Each modality was evaluated as a standalone baseline, and fusion was defined as concatenation of the selected expression, copy-number, and mutation blocks. All models used the same Ridge estimator and alpha value, isolating the effect of adding blocks rather than changing the learner. Ridge is treated as a reproducible reference, not as an optimal architecture.
0072	
0073	## 2.6. Split manifests and reproducibility
0074	
0075	Five drug-held-out manifests were persisted before model evaluation. Grouped splitting prevents rows from the same drug from appearing in both training and test partitions. Leave-one-dataset-out manifests hold out one complete response source at a time. Result JSON files retain seeds, row counts, selected-feature counts, model labels, and error metrics.
0076	
0077	## 2.7. Analysis sequence and implementation controls
0078	
0079	The analysis sequence was fixed before interpreting performance: define the 15-compound universe; normalize identifiers; construct outer-joined tables for availability auditing; create grouped manifests; select features using training rows only; fit training-derived imputation and scaling; fit the same Ridge specification; and calculate held-out MAE and RMSE. No test response values were used for feature ranking, imputation, scaling, or model selection. Earlier candidate-table and cell-line-held-out analyses are exploratory history and are not headline results.
0080	
0081	# 3. Results
0082	
0083	### 3.1. Drug-heldout validation
0084	
0085	Drug-held-out performance was small and seed-sensitive across five full-universe splits. Mean RMSE across seeds was 0.2322 for fusion versus 0.2328 for expression alone; this is an arithmetic mean of split-level RMSE values, not a pooled observation-level RMSE. Per-seed and per-model values are reported in Table 2 and Supplementary Table S3.
0086	
0087	As a secondary diagnostic, a training-set mean-response baseline was worse than expression and fusion in four of five seeds; in the fifth seed it was better than both. A per-drug audit covered eight unique held-out compounds across 15 drug-seed cells; fusion improved RMSE in 11 cells and worsened it in four. Because compounds recur across seeds and only three drugs are held out per split, this audit is descriptive rather than an independent estimate of generalization.
0088	
0089	### 3.2. Cross-study transfer
0090	
0091	In leave-one-dataset-out validation, expression was consistently stronger than fusion, with the largest fusion degradation on CTD2. These folds test transfer across source datasets, not replication of the same drug. The signed fusion-minus-expression RMSE deltas are shown in Figure 2 and Supplementary Table S4.
0092	
0093	The magnitude of the transfer differences should not be compared as if the three datasets shared a common response scale. I therefore emphasize the within-fold fusion-minus-expression contrast and report the raw metrics only to make each fold auditable. The magnitude of the transfer differences should be read together with the source-specific row counts and response construction. The held-out datasets are not interchangeable partitions of one identical experiment: they differ in compound composition, cell-line coverage, assay procedures, and the availability of molecular blocks. Thus, the LOOD comparison asks whether the complete modeling pipeline transfers to a new response source, not whether the same drug effect is reproduced in a second assay. It is a stress test for portability, but it cannot isolate whether poorer fusion performance arises from multimodal integration, source shift, compound composition, assay differences, response-scale differences, or interactions among these factors. I therefore treat the observed degradation as an association with the transfer setting, not as evidence of a causal incompatibility of multimodal models.
0094	
0095	### 3.3. Missingness and redundancy
0096	
0097	Row-level modality summaries showed weak-to-moderate correlations, while outer-joined tables showed dataset-dependent modality availability. These analyses describe data structure and model behavior; they do not establish that missingness or redundancy caused the observed performance differences.
0098	
0099	The outer-join audit found mean availability of 0.827 for expression, 0.594 for copy number, and 0.995 for damaging mutation, with 0.591 of rows complete across all three blocks. These values are calculated from the prepared outer-joined tables before fold-specific feature selection. They describe the 15-compound tables and selected cell-line intersection, not complete-case availability of the selected fold-specific 2,000-feature blocks, portal-wide availability, or clinical assay availability. Models retained incomplete rows through training-fold median imputation.

0101	# 4. Discussion
0102	
0103	The results do not support a universal multimodal advantage. Fusion can help within a drug-held-out universe yet perform worse within the observed study-transfer setting, consistent with domain-specific response scales, assay coverage, feature distributions, and other simultaneous source differences. This pattern is compatible with prior reports that multimodal integration can improve prediction under particular data and validation settings [5,8], while benchmarking work shows variation across algorithms, drugs, and transfer targets [9,10]. My controlled comparison adds a more modest observation: the incremental fusion effect is small within the declared drug-held-out benchmark and reverses direction under source-dataset transfer. This does not refute multimodal modeling; it shows why a gain observed in one validation regime should not be generalized to another without direct testing. The study is limited by the small prespecified 15-drug universe, limited same-drug overlap across datasets, and the use of compact Ridge baselines rather than a broad model class.
0104	
0105	H1 was only partially supported in the full-universe drug-heldout benchmark: the mean favored fusion, but only three of five seed-level comparisons did so. The LOOD contrast was less favorable for fusion in all three held-out sources, but this is an observation about the combined transfer setting rather than support for H2 as a causal or general law. H3 was supported descriptively because modality availability and row-level correlations differed across source tables, but the study does not identify a causal explanation. These findings argue for reporting effect size and transfer failures alongside the direction of the fusion comparison.
0106	
0107	The methodological implication is that validation design is part of the scientific claim. A random cell-line-drug split can estimate interpolation within an existing response system, whereas a drug-held-out split in this study asks whether a cell-line-only relationship transfers to held-out compound records within the declared universe. Because the model has no drug-related input, this is not conventional arbitrary-unseen-drug prediction. A leave-one-dataset-out split adds a different stress test: it asks whether the learned relationship survives a change in source, assay, and coverage. These estimands should not be conflated. My study uses them sequentially to show that the answer to “does fusion help?” depends on which generalization target is intended.
0108	
0109	The small size of the observed within-universe effect is itself informative for model development. The mean difference is much smaller than the variation in absolute RMSE across seeds, and the direction changes in two of five comparisons. This pattern does not justify selecting fusion solely because its arithmetic mean is lower. A reasonable decision rule would also consider the cost of obtaining and maintaining the extra modalities, the completeness of those modalities in the intended population, and whether the improvement is reproduced under a prespecified external test. Under the present evidence, expression remains a competitive reference and fusion is best treated as a candidate extension requiring further validation.
0110	
0111	The analysis also suggests a reproducibility principle for future information-fusion studies: preserve the split manifests and the feature-selection scope as first-class artifacts. Without them, a later rerun can silently change the estimand while retaining the same model name and headline metric. Reporting the sign of each fold-level delta, rather than only an aggregate score, makes such changes easier to detect. This is particularly important when the expected effect is small, as in the present benchmark.
0112	
0113	# 5. Conclusion
0114	
0115	In this small, prespecified DepMap benchmark, adding copy-number and mutation blocks to expression produced a small mean improvement in drug-held-out prediction, but the direction changed across repeated splits and the improvement did not transfer to any of the three held-out source datasets. The result is therefore conditional rather than universal. The practical contribution is a reproducible comparison framework that makes the held-out entity, fold-local feature selection, structured availability, and transfer target explicit. Larger common-drug universes, external test sets, missingness-aware models, and alternative learners are needed before deciding whether the observed pattern generalizes beyond this Ridge-based construction.
